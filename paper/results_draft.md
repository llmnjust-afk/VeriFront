# Results (draft v0.1 — number anchors verified against repo reports; citations to be added at Intro/Related stage)

## Replacement damage concentrates at identifiable steps

Task-level pass rates conflate two questions that must be separated before model allocation can be reasoned about: whether an agent can solve a task, and *where* in its execution it genuinely requires frontier capability. We therefore froze complete trajectories produced by a frontier model on scientific-computing tasks and re-executed them from individual steps, replacing only the policy at that step while holding context, tool state, and remaining budget fixed (Fig. 1; Methods). Across 705 in-point runs spanning 38 step instances, four replacement arms — frontier resampling and three cheaper edit models (two API-tier models, mini and DeepSeek, and an open-weights Qwen3-14B) — we asked a simple question at each step: does the cheaper model, given exactly the same situation, reproduce what the frontier model did?

The answer varies far more across steps than across tasks. Difference-in-differences against matched control steps isolates the step-specific component of replacement damage: relative to their behaviour on ordinary steps, cheaper models lose 0.21 (mini), 0.25 (DeepSeek) and 0.71 (Qwen) success probability at the six identified signal steps (pooled controls; the ordering is preserved under a common-control-set calibration: −0.24, −0.27, −0.63). That the largest open-weights model in our sweep suffers the most step-specific damage is not a paradox of capability but a signature of behaviour: error-family coding of the replacement-step transcripts shows that Qwen's signal-step failures are dominated by a model-idiosyncratic error family (25 of 52 runs) that neither API model produces at scale, whereas the API models fail predominantly by emitting no code at all (protocol lapses) and the frontier resamples essentially never err at the step level (Fig. 3b). Failure at a step is thus not a graded attenuation of frontier behaviour but a discrete, model-characteristic event — an observation that motivates treating steps, not tasks, as the unit of analysis.

## Full-step profiles separate healthy, capability-dependent and cliff steps

Aggregating over single signal points risks missing structure that only a dense scan can reveal. We therefore swept every step of two representative parent trajectories (t40, five pre-deliverable steps; t60, nine steps) across all four arms under the same counterfactual protocol, yielding 193 sandbox-valid runs and a complete per-step, per-arm success-rate surface (Fig. 2). Three step classes emerge from the resample baseline alone. *Healthy* steps (resample success 1.0) tolerate every replacement arm within confidence limits. *Capability-dependent* steps show graded degradation that tracks model capacity. *Cliff* steps — where resampling itself fails — behave discontinuously: at t60s2, t60s3 and t60s6, no arm recovers more than a fraction of frontier performance, and at the single-step replacement level mini reaches only 0.14–0.50.

Two cautions temper this surface. First, per-cell replication remains small enough that no individual step-arm contrast survives BH-FDR correction at q < 0.10 across the 42 tests of the scan snapshot — the informative content of the scan is the *profile shape* (concentration and classification), not any single cell. Second, the cliff class is defined by the frontier baseline's own failure, so it identifies steps whose difficulty is intrinsic rather than model-relative. Both cautions are addressed by the aggregation and intervention analyses below, which is why we position the scan as descriptive anatomy rather than confirmatory evidence.

## Structured feedback repairs; naive splicing amplifies

If step-level failures are discrete events rather than graded attenuation, they should respond to targeted intervention — and respond differently to interventions that respect versus violate step boundaries. We tested three interventions at the signal steps (Fig. 3c). First, structured feedback: when a replacement step fails, re-prompting the edit model with a structured diagnosis of the failure (error class, expected output contract, failing assertion) raises frontier-step success from 0.54 under bare retry to 0.75, and mini's from 0.33 to 0.42, while consuming fewer retry rounds (1 of 12 versus 3 of 13). The repair is partial rather than complete, consistent with a residual component of genuine capability gap that feedback cannot substitute. Second, suffix takeover — splicing the cheaper model's edit into the frontier trajectory and letting the frontier model continue from there — *amplifies* damage rather than containing it: mini's success falls from 0.33 (single-step replacement) to 0.14 at t40s4 and from 0.38 to 0.00 at t60s4. The information a frontier model needs to recover from a foreign edit is evidently not recoverable from the edit's surface form; step boundaries are not clean interfaces for heterogeneous policies. Third, scale alone does not dissolve the failure: doubling Qwen from 8B to 14B leaves pooled signal-point success statistically indistinguishable (0.37 versus 0.45, Fisher p = 0.61), with two signal points repaired, two persisting and two newly failing — the idiosyncratic error family migrates rather than vanishes.

A final control concerns the experimental machinery itself: runs that exhaust the step budget are not silently counted as failures but re-executed under a protocol retry, and their final success rate (4 of 8) matches matched controls (4 of 8), confirming that budget exhaustion is an artifact of the observation window rather than a property of the replaced steps.

## Blind-coded verifiability features predict step-level replaceability

The preceding results establish *that* frontier dependence concentrates and *how* it fails; they do not yet say whether one could have predicted where, from properties of the step alone. We posed this as a pre-registered prediction problem. Two annotators — one of them an AI assistant with declared contamination limits, one the project lead, both blind to all per-point replacement outcomes — independently coded seven verifiability features for each of the 38 step instances from frozen reading materials (task instruction, step code, truncated observation, step transcript) generated by a script with machine checks against leakage. Inter-annotator agreement passed for the two features with mechanical definitions (weighted κ = 0.83 for artifact production, 1.00 for step type) and fell below 0.6 for the five judgement-laden features, triggering a pre-registered two-round clarification and adjudication protocol over a {0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2} value grid; the merged 38 × 7 matrix (128 cells by agreement, 81 by one-level averaging, 17 by adjudication, 2 by annotator-A default) was frozen with a timestamp before any per-point outcome was examined.

Under task-level leave-one-out cross-validation with fixed regularisation (no tuning leakage), a ridge model adding the six feature scores to step position, remaining budget, stratum, role and arm identity reduces prediction error of per-arm success rate by 0.036 RMSE and raises Spearman correlation from 0.30 to 0.47 — meeting both pre-registered criteria (ΔRMSE ≤ 0, Δρ ≥ 0.1) for the hypothesis that verifiability carries independent predictive signal (Fig. 4). A gradient-boosting sensitivity check reproduces the direction. The gain is not uniform: it concentrates on the API arms and is driven largely by the distinction between exploratory steps — which lack an objective correctness standard and are therefore unusually safe to delegate to cheaper models — and deliverable-producing steps, which are not. For the open-weights arm, whose failure signature is nearly collinear with its identity, features add nothing beyond the arm indicator, an honest boundary on what verifiability profiling can and cannot do. With 38 step instances the result is directional rather than definitive, and we report it with the pre-registered power caveat attached; the F2 artifact measure is an operational proxy for deliverable production, not a claim that agents possess semantic verifiers at runtime.
