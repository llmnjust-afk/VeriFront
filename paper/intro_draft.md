## Abstract

Scientific agents built on large language models are increasingly deployed across real research workflows, yet remain evaluated at the level of whole tasks: a pass rate tells us *whether* an agent succeeds but not *where* in its execution frontier capability is genuinely required. We introduce step-level counterfactual replacement — surgically substituting the policy at a single step of a frozen agent trajectory while holding context, tool state and remaining budget fixed — and apply it to scientific-computing agents executing multi-step data-analysis tasks. Across 767 controlled runs spanning four replacement policies (frontier resampling, two API-tier models and open-weights models), replacement damage concentrates at a minority of steps: relative to matched control steps, cheaper models lose 0.21–0.71 success probability at identified signal steps, and the damage is model-specific rather than graded, splitting into protocol lapses, semantic errors and model-idiosyncratic error families. Full-step scans separate healthy, capability-dependent and cliff steps; structured feedback partially repairs failures (+0.21 success rate) whereas naive step-splicing amplifies them, and doubling model scale does not dissolve the failures. Finally, two annotators blind to all outcomes code verifiability features of each step from pre-registered materials; these features predict step-level replaceability under task-level cross-validation (Δρ +0.17 over controls), meeting pre-registered criteria. Verifiability profiling turns "which model for which step" from anecdote into measurement, and offers a design principle for allocating frontier capability in scientific agents.

## Introduction

Large language-model agents have moved from demonstration to deployment in scientific research itself: systems that plan and execute multi-step analyses now contribute to genome-scale model construction, autonomous chemistry, and end-to-end research pipelines [qian2026towards;xie2026autonomous;kong2026autoresearch]. This deployment rests on an architectural habit — a single frontier policy model drives most or all reasoning steps — even though the capability distribution across steps is anything but uniform. Steps that explore a dataset, check an intermediate result, or format an artifact rarely demand the same reasoning depth as steps that must synthesize a flawed plan into a working deliverable, and the efficiency literature already shows that smaller models outperform larger ones on many narrow, well-specified subtasks [cao2026taskspecific;garg2025emissions]. Meanwhile the practical machinery of agent systems — memory, skills, harnesses, orchestration layers — has grown enormously more sophisticated [zhou2026externalization;wei2026survey]. What has not grown is any principled answer to the allocation question this progress begs: *which steps of a scientific agent actually need the frontier model, and how would one know?*

Existing evaluation practice cannot answer it. The dominant benchmarks score agents at the task level — repository issue resolution, scientific workflows, equation discovery — and report a single aggregate success rate [yang2024sweagent;deng2025swebench;zhang2025swebench;sun2025scienceboard;shojaee2025llmsrbench;fang2025mlzero]. Task-level scores are silent about step-level structure: an agent at 40% may be uniformly mediocre or excellent-except-one-step, and these two profiles demand opposite interventions. Where judges are needed to score open-ended steps, LLM-as-a-judge evaluation introduces its own reliability-validity problems [gu2024survey;wei2024systematic;norman2026reliability]. A parallel literature on process supervision scores individual reasoning steps — process reward models, step-level verification, and critiques of reward hacking show the appetite for step-level signals [ma2024what;zhang2025groundedprm;pronesti2026beyond;zhang2026when;nie2026stop] — but these scores are *learned* judgments, typically distilled from models or humans, and inherit the circularity they are meant to resolve: the verifier is another model whose own competence boundary is unknown. Failure-attribution tools locate *which* step failed post hoc [deng2026defa], yet attribution is not intervention: nothing in it establishes what would have happened had a different policy executed that step. To our knowledge, no existing protocol measures, by direct experiment, the causal consequence of substituting the model at one step while holding everything else fixed.

We close this gap with **step-level counterfactual replacement**. The protocol freezes a complete trajectory produced by a frontier policy model on a scientific-computing task, selects a step, and re-executes the trajectory from that step with one substitution — the step's action is produced by a different model, from an identical prompt, in an identical sandbox, with identical remaining budget — while all other steps retain the frontier policy. Success is judged only by objective executors (unit tests, file assertions, programmatic checks), never by model opinion, and every replacement arm is paired with a frontier-resampling arm that controls for stochasticity of re-execution itself. The design inherits the intervention logic of counterfactual evaluation while targeting the step, the natural unit at which agent frameworks are actually composed and at which model routing decisions must ultimately be made [li2026progrouter;sun2026cborchestrator]. Because outcomes are objective, the protocol yields ground truth that process-reward approaches approximate and judge-based evaluation cannot certify.

Applying the protocol to scientific data-analysis agents — 767 controlled runs over 38 step instances across four replacement policies — yields three findings. First, replacement damage is concentrated and heterogeneous: difference-in-differences against matched control steps attributes step-specific losses of 0.21 (mini), 0.25 (DeepSeek) and 0.71 (Qwen-14B) to six signal steps, and error-family coding shows the losses arise by different mechanisms — protocol lapses for API models, a model-idiosyncratic error family for the open-weights model — rather than by uniform capability shortfall. Second, dense scans of complete trajectories separate steps into healthy, capability-dependent and cliff classes, and interventions validate the taxonomy: structured feedback repairs a substantial fraction of failures (+0.21), suffix takeover — splicing a cheaper edit and letting the frontier model continue — amplifies damage, and doubling open-weights model size does not dissolve step-level failures. Third, and most constructively, seven verifiability features of a step — coded blind to all replacement outcomes from frozen reading materials, with pre-registered adjudication and a frozen timestamp — predict step-level replaceability under task-level cross-validation, meeting both pre-registered criteria (ΔRMSE ≤ 0, Δρ ≥ 0.1). Steps lacking an objective correctness standard are reliably the safest to delegate to cheaper models; deliverable-producing steps are not.

Our contributions are:

- a **measurement protocol** — step-level counterfactual replacement with objective adjudication and resampling controls — that turns step-level model dependence into an experimentally measurable quantity rather than a judged one;
- an **anatomy of frontier dependence** in scientific agents: concentration at identifiable steps, a three-class step taxonomy, and mechanism-level evidence that failures are discrete, model-characteristic events (protocol lapses, idiosyncratic error families) rather than graded attenuation;
- **intervention results** that bound repair strategies: structured feedback helps, naive splicing hurts, and scale alone does not fix step failures;
- a **pre-registered, blind-coded prediction test** showing that verifiability features carry independent signal about where cheaper models suffice, establishing verifiability as a measurable design principle for allocating frontier capability in agent pipelines.

Section [results] reports the four result blocks; Methods specifies the protocol, the annotation and prediction designs, and all pre-registrations; the Discussion locates the findings against process supervision and model routing, and states the limits of a single-skeleton, single-domain, 38-point study.

## Discussion

**Verifiability as a measurable orchestration principle.** The routing and orchestration literature asks which model should serve which request and answers with learned cost-quality predictors or bandit feedback [li2026progrouter;sun2026cborchestrator;koul2026register;cao2026taskspecific]; our results supply the ground truth such predictors ultimately need, and suggest that for scientific agents a large share of the answer is available *before* any routing data is collected — from the epistemic structure of the step itself. A step that produces an artifact with an objective correctness standard is predictable in its dependence; a step whose quality cannot be objectively adjudicated is safe to delegate precisely because nothing there requires frontier reasoning. This reframing matters for practice: verification-oriented scaffolds and test-generation agents are already being built [lee2025learning;kapetanovic2026phased], and our measurement protocol provides the missing evaluation layer for them — not "does the small model feel adequate" but "does the small model, under identical context, reproduce frontier outcomes at rates that matter". The finding that naive splicing amplifies damage adds a cautionary boundary: step boundaries in current agent frameworks are not clean interfaces, and hybrid frontier/cheap pipelines must be engineered around recovery information, not assumed to compose.

**Relation to process supervision.** Process reward models and step-level verifiers pursue the same granularity by *learning* step scores from data [zhang2025groundedprm;zhang2025linking;rahman2025scaleprm;samarinas2026truncated;liu2026confspec;zhou2026silr], with recent work documenting both reward hacking and faithfulness failures of learned verifiers [ma2024what;nie2026stop;zhang2026when]. Our protocol occupies the complementary extreme: in an environment where success is objectively executable, step-level ground truth can be *measured* rather than learned, at the price of executing the counterfactual. The two approaches are not competitors but stages of a pipeline — measured replacement outcomes of the kind we produce are exactly the training signal that learned process models lack, and our blind-coded features are exactly the cheap proxies such models could be distilled from. The idiosyncratic error family we observe (a frontier-quality open-weights model failing signal steps in ways neither API model does) also carries a message for the verifier literature: step failures are not draws from a shared difficulty distribution, and verifiers trained on one model's failures may be structurally blind to another's — a heterogeneity that aggregate pass-rate evaluation cannot surface.

**Limits and what would falsify the picture.** Our evidence comes from one agent skeleton, one task domain (scientific computing on tabular data), and 38 pre-registered step instances; per-cell statistical power is modest enough that no individual step-arm contrast survives multiple-comparison correction, and the headline claims rest on aggregations (difference-in-differences, error-family counts, cross-validated prediction) rather than single contrasts — with the 38-point prediction result explicitly directional against a pre-registered 60-point target. The verifiability features are blind-coded but human-defined, one (artifact production) is an operational proxy rather than a semantic property, and one annotator is an AI assistant whose contamination we bounded but could not eliminate. The strongest test of the framework's generality — predicting replaceability on a *new* task domain whose steps were annotated under the same frozen protocol — remains future work, as does closing the loop from prediction to live orchestration decisions. Within these bounds, the picture is coherent and actionable: frontier capability in scientific agents is a step-level, mechanistically heterogeneous, and partially predictable quantity, and the tools to measure it cost roughly a hundred dollars of compute — orders of magnitude below the cost of the deployments whose design they should inform. Evaluating agents at that resolution is, we argue, the cheapest available upgrade to the science of agent engineering, and a necessary one if the field is to keep its claims about "where intelligence is needed" honest [beel2025evaluating;miyai2025scientist;emami2026position].
