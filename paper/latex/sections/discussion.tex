\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Verifiability as a measurable orchestration principle.} The routing and orchestration literature asks which model should serve which request and answers with learned cost-quality predictors or bandit feedback \cite{li2026progrouter,sun2026cborchestrator,koul2026register,cao2026taskspecific}; our results supply the ground truth such predictors ultimately need, and suggest that for scientific agents a large share of the answer is available \emph{before} any routing data is collected --- from the epistemic structure of the step itself. Within the annotated distribution, a step that produces an artifact with an objective correctness standard is predictable in its dependence, and a step whose quality cannot be objectively adjudicated is comparatively safe to delegate; our held-out check (Results) shows this regularity does not yet extend to unlabelled instances, so the principle currently licenses annotation-guided allocation, not untrained automatic routing. This reframing matters for practice: verification-oriented scaffolds and test-generation agents are already being built \cite{lee2025learning,kapetanovic2026phased}, domain-specific agent stacks for materials discovery expose the same frontier-dependence question in another guise \cite{rothfarb2025hierarchical}, component-level ablations of data-science agent workflows show how much of end-to-end performance is attributable to individual modules \cite{huang2026llmgenerated}, and surveys of autonomous discovery systems converge on orchestration as the open problem \cite{zhou2025autonomous}; our measurement protocol provides the missing evaluation layer for all of these --- not "does the small model feel adequate" but "does the small model, under identical context, reproduce frontier outcomes at rates that matter". The finding that naive splicing amplifies damage adds a cautionary boundary: step boundaries in current agent frameworks are not clean interfaces, and hybrid frontier/cheap pipelines must be engineered around recovery information, not assumed to compose.

\textbf{Relation to process supervision.} Process reward models and step-level verifiers pursue the same granularity by \emph{learning} step scores from data \cite{zhang2025groundedprm,zhang2025linking,rahman2025scaleprm,samarinas2026truncated,liu2026confspec,zhou2026silr}, with recent work documenting both reward hacking and faithfulness failures of learned verifiers \cite{ma2024what,nie2026stop,zhang2026when}. Our protocol occupies the complementary extreme: in an environment where success is objectively executable, step-level ground truth can be \emph{measured} rather than learned, at the price of executing the counterfactual. The two approaches are not competitors but stages of a pipeline --- measured replacement outcomes of the kind we produce are exactly the training signal that learned process models lack, and our blind-coded features are exactly the cheap proxies such models could be distilled from --- complements to rubric-based reward ranking and dense verifiable-reward designs that enrich the reward signal without a learned verifier \cite{li2026rlvr,wang2026beyond}. The idiosyncratic error family we observe (a frontier-quality open-weights model failing signal steps in ways neither API model does) also carries a message for the verifier literature: step failures are not draws from a shared difficulty distribution, and verifiers trained on one model's failures may be structurally blind to another's --- a heterogeneity that aggregate pass-rate evaluation cannot surface. Our held-out check adds the complementary warning: blind-coded features, like learned verifiers, fail to transfer outside their sampling regime, so neither route yields a free out-of-distribution step scorer.

\textbf{Limits and what would falsify the picture.} Our evidence comes from one agent skeleton, one task domain (scientific computing on tabular data), and 38 pre-registered step instances; per-cell statistical power is modest enough that no individual step-arm contrast survives multiple-comparison correction, and the headline claims rest on aggregations (difference-in-differences, error-family counts, cross-validated prediction) rather than single contrasts --- with the 38-point prediction result explicitly directional against a pre-registered 60-point target. The verifiability features are blind-coded but human-defined, one (artifact production) is an operational proxy rather than a semantic property, one annotator is an AI assistant whose contamination we bounded but could not eliminate, and the held-out validation shows the cross-validated gain does not transfer to unlabelled instances at either the instance or the task level. The strongest test of the framework's generality --- predicting replaceability on a \emph{new} task domain whose steps were annotated under the same frozen protocol --- remains future work, as does closing the loop from prediction to live orchestration decisions. Within these bounds, the picture is coherent and actionable: frontier capability in scientific agents is a step-level, mechanistically heterogeneous, and partially predictable quantity, and the tools to measure it cost roughly a hundred dollars of compute --- orders of magnitude below the cost of the deployments whose design they should inform. Evaluating agents at that resolution is, we argue, the cheapest available upgrade to the science of agent engineering, and a necessary one if the field is to keep its claims about "where intelligence is needed" honest \cite{beel2025evaluating,miyai2025scientist,emami2026position}.
